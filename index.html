1 · The problem

Deciding what flies when the next ship is two years away

Every rocket to an off-world base has a fixed mass and volume, and in 2035 every flight has more cargo waiting than room. Someone has to choose what goes. On Earth that choice is cheap to get wrong, because the next truck leaves tomorrow. To Mars, the next ship leaves in about 26 months, so the manifest decides who goes without for two years.

Today manifests for a handful of government missions are built by hand, in spreadsheets and meetings. That stops working once several bases and dozens of companies share each flight. Three things break at once:

  • Fairness: there are no shared, published rules for who gets bumped, so money or politics decides.
  • Foresight: nobody sees that a bumped load leaves a base short until the ship lands without it.
  • Requests: crews ask over a delayed link, and every clarifying question to Mars costs up to 44 minutes.

3 · Why it matters

A bumped load can be a health risk for two years

  • The stakes are lives, not delays. A CO₂ scrubber cartridge that misses the Mars window leaves a crew short of air-cleaning spares until the next one.
  • Every kilogram is expensive. Delivering cargo to the Moon costs hundreds of thousands of dollars per kilogram today. Wasted room is wasted money.
  • Sharing needs trust. Agencies and companies will only share flights if the rules are fair, published and the same for everyone.
  • Bases can't fix it alone. By the time a crew notices a part is missing, the window has closed.

Why today's tools won't cope

  • Freight software optimises cost or revenue. A missed slot on Earth costs a day.
  • Airline cargo bumps by fare. Off Earth, the highest payer must never outrank a CO₂ scrubber.
  • Planning tools assume a live link to one central system. Mars is 22 minutes away and sometimes unreachable.

Why our answer holds up

  • The 26-month window and the signal delay are physics, not guesses.
  • The first step, commercial lunar cargo, already exists.
  • The problem grows with every base and every company that joins.

5 · What the solution does

Ask, decide, show the cost, agree

Request desk

AI

Crews write what they need in their own words. Claude turns the message into a booking draft, works out the need date from what's left in stock, and puts every missing detail into one reply.

Packer

RULES

Published, fixed rules decide what flies: life-critical first, then mission-critical, then commercial by bid. Money only sorts the commercial tier.

Consequence board

RULES

Every load is checked against the date its base needs it. Each manifest is measured in days a base goes short, with late loads in red, months before launch.

Manifest fingerprint

RULES

An 8-character code computed from each manifest. Earth and the base compare codes in one short message to confirm they hold the same plan.

Briefs

AI

Claude drafts the note to the commander and the message to each bumped shipper, using only the packer's numbers. A person checks it and sends it.

Left out on purpose

  • Blockchain: one operator with signed bookings is enough. A chain adds cost and slow agreement between planets.
  • An optimisation solver or machine learning for packing: a commander can't check its answers. Fixed rules can be checked step by step.
  • A server database: the prototype keeps its state in the browser. Production adds a shared store and signatures.

7 · Design and build

AI reads and writes. Rules decide. People approve.

Never guess a number

A missing mass or date stays missing and blocks the booking. The page checks every field Claude returns.

Same inputs, same manifest

No randomness and no AI in the decision, so Earth and the base can each compute it and agree.

Measure what people feel

A manifest is scored by days a base goes short, not by kilograms carried or revenue earned.

A person signs off

Claude drafts bookings and messages. Nothing is booked or sent without a cargo officer.

  1. PEOPLECrew writes a requestIn plain words, under pressure, over a delayed link.
  2. AIClaude drafts the bookingLoad, class, mass, volume, need date. Missing facts become questions.
  3. PEOPLECargo officer approvesMissing numbers block it. Nothing is guessed.
  4. RULESPacker builds manifestsFlight by flight, in a fixed order, one reason per decision.
  5. RULESBoard shows the costArrival against need date, days a base goes short, fingerprint.
  6. AIClaude drafts the briefsThe officer edits and sends them to the base and each shipper.
for each base:
  waiting = every booking for that base
  for each flight to that base, in launch order:
    sort waiting by class: life-critical, mission-critical, commercial
      inside life and mission: earliest need date first
      inside commercial: highest bid per kg first
    for each load in that order:
      if it fits the mass and volume left: put it on this flight
      otherwise: it waits for the next flight
for each load:
  arrives  = launch + trip days
  days short = arrives - need date   (late if above zero)
  1. Life-critical loads fly first, earliest need first. Food, air, water and medicine are never bumped by anything else.
  2. Mission-critical loads go next, earliest need first: power, greenhouses, repairs.
  3. Commercial loads fill the space that's left, highest bid per kilogram first. A bid never lifts a load into a higher class.
  4. A load that doesn't fit moves to the next flight to the same base, where the same rules run again.
  5. Every load is checked against its need date. Late loads show how long the base goes short and who is affected.

    Five minutes of questions

    What the jury will ask

    What did you create that wasn't already there?

    Days a base goes short as the measure of a manifest; a class order where bids only sort the commercial tier; a deterministic packer with a fingerprint so two planets can agree without negotiating; and a request desk built for delay that asks every missing question at once.

    Why not let the AI decide what flies?

    Decisions about air and food must be predictable and explainable. The packer gives the same manifest for the same inputs and shows each step. That also lets Earth and the base run it separately and still agree, which an AI decision can't promise.

    What if Claude reads a request wrong?

    It only makes a draft. The page checks every field: an unknown base, a missing number or a date in the past becomes "Missing" and blocks the booking. A cargo officer approves every booking. If Claude isn't available, the offline reader does the same job with simpler rules.

    How does the fingerprint work?

    It is an FNV-1a hash of the flight and its loads in packing order, shown as 8 characters. If Earth and Ares hold the same bookings, they compute the same code. Comparing codes takes one short message instead of resending the manifest. A real system would also sign the bookings.

    Why greedy packing instead of an optimal solver?

    Exact packing is a knapsack problem: it gets expensive as loads grow and its choices are hard to explain. Greedy by class runs instantly and every decision has one reason. An exact solver could later run as a second opinion.

    Can a rich company buy its way to the top?

    No. The class comes from what the load does and is set by base policy. Bids only order loads inside the commercial class.

    What if the life-critical loads alone don't fit?

    They move to the next flight like any load and turn red straight away, with how many days the base goes short. Software can't add room to a rocket. It makes sure a person sees the problem months ahead.

    Where do the 26 months come from?

    Earth and Mars line up for a low-energy transfer about every 780 days. Launching outside that window needs far more fuel, so cargo flights cluster in the windows.

    Who pays for it?

    Flight operators license it to plan and sell capacity, base agencies use the desk and board, and there is a small fee on each commercial booking.

    What are the limits?

    Mass and volume only: no centre of gravity, hazardous goods, partial loads or return cargo. Prices, companies and capacities are invented. Launch timing follows the real cycle.
